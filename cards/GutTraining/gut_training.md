Q: What is gut training in endurance sports?
A: Deliberately practicing high-carbohydrate intake during workouts to adapt the gut to absorb and tolerate more calories per hour, reducing GI distress in races.

---

Q: What is the baseline carbohydrate absorption limit without gut training?
A: ~60 g/hr, limited by the SGLT1 transporter that handles glucose and maltose.

---

Q: What is the maximum carbohydrate absorption rate achievable with gut training using multiple transportable carbs?
A: Up to 90–120 g/hr, sometimes higher in highly trained athletes.

---

Q: What are the two main intestinal transporters for carbohydrates?
A: SGLT1 (sodium-glucose linked transporter 1) for glucose/maltose, and GLUT5 for fructose.

---

C: Because glucose and fructose use [different transporters], combining them allows total carbohydrate absorption to exceed the 60 g/hr single-transporter ceiling.

---

Q: What is the optimal glucose-to-fructose ratio for maximum carbohydrate absorption?
A: 2:1 (glucose:fructose), which fully saturates both SGLT1 and GLUT5 transporters simultaneously.

---

Q: Why does a 2:1 glucose:fructose ratio outperform glucose alone at high intakes?
A: Glucose alone saturates SGLT1 at ~60 g/hr; adding fructose recruits GLUT5 in parallel, increasing total throughput without competing for the same transporter.

---

Q: How does gut training physiologically adapt the intestine?
A: It upregulates transporter density (SGLT1, GLUT5), increases intestinal surface area perfusion, and trains the stomach to empty faster at higher volumes.

---

Q: How long does it typically take to meaningfully adapt gut absorption through training?
A: 4–8 weeks of consistent high-carb fueling during workouts.

---

Q: What is the general calorie target per hour for events lasting 2–4 hours?
A: 60–90 g/hr of carbohydrate (~240–360 kcal/hr), using a 2:1 glucose:fructose source.

---

Q: What is the general calorie target per hour for ultra-endurance events (>4 hours)?
A: 90–120 g/hr of carbohydrate (~360–480 kcal/hr), achievable only with multi-transporter carb sources and trained gut.

---

C: The practical rule is: never try a new fueling strategy on race day. [Train the gut] in workouts that mirror race intensity and duration.

---

Q: What is gastric emptying and why does it matter for gut training?
A: Gastric emptying is the rate at which the stomach delivers food to the small intestine. Faster emptying allows more substrate to reach absorptive transporters, increasing the effective calorie ceiling.

---

Q: What factors slow gastric emptying during exercise?
A: High exercise intensity (>70–75% VO2max), high-fat or high-fiber content, hyperosmotic solutions, and dehydration.

---

Q: Why should gut training sessions be performed at race-representative intensity?
A: Gastric emptying and gut perfusion are intensity-dependent; training at lower intensity does not fully replicate the GI stress of race pace.

---

Q: What is the progressive overload principle applied to gut training?
A: Start at a tolerable carbohydrate intake (e.g., 40–60 g/hr) and increase by 10–15 g/hr every 1–2 weeks until reaching the target race intake.

---

Q: Name three common GI symptoms caused by unadapted high-carb intake during exercise.
A: Nausea, bloating/cramping, and diarrhea (runner's gut).

---

Q: Why is liquid carbohydrate generally better tolerated than solid food at high exercise intensities?
A: Liquids empty from the stomach faster and require less digestive work, keeping more blood flow available to working muscles rather than the gut.

---

Q: What role does sodium play in carbohydrate absorption in the gut?
A: SGLT1 is a sodium-glucose cotransporter; sodium is co-transported with glucose, so adequate sodium in the solution drives faster glucose uptake.

---

C: Sports drinks that use [sodium] alongside glucose leverage the SGLT1 co-transport mechanism, enhancing absorption rate compared to plain glucose solutions.

---

Q: What is the osmolality target for an easily absorbed sports drink?
A: Isotonic to hypotonic (~200–290 mOsm/kg). Hypertonic solutions (>300 mOsm/kg) draw water into the gut lumen and slow absorption.

---

Q: How does heat and humidity affect gut absorption capacity during exercise?
A: Heat stress redirects blood flow to the skin for cooling, reducing splanchnic (gut) blood flow and decreasing absorptive capacity and gastric emptying rate.

---

Q: What is the recommended carbohydrate intake for workouts shorter than 60 minutes?
A: Gut training is generally not needed; water is sufficient. Focus gut training sessions on rides/runs ≥90 minutes.

---

Q: Give two example carbohydrate sources with a natural ~2:1 glucose:fructose ratio.
A: Maltodextrin + fructose blends (most race gels/chews) and sucrose (table sugar), which is a 1:1 glucose:fructose disaccharide that digests to ~equal parts.

---

Q: What is the "carbohydrate mouth rinse" technique and when is it useful?
A: Swishing a carbohydrate solution in the mouth for ~10 seconds without swallowing; activates oral receptors to reduce perceived effort in short (<60 min) intense efforts when gut load is undesirable.

---

Q: How does fat ingestion affect carbohydrate absorption during exercise?
A: Fat slows gastric emptying significantly, delaying carbohydrate delivery to the intestine and reducing the effective absorption rate per hour.

---

C: For maximum calorie throughput during racing, [minimize fat and fiber] intake in the hours before and during the event to keep gastric emptying fast.

---

Q: What is the practical gut training protocol for an Ironman or ultra-marathon?
A: In long training sessions (≥2 hr), consume carbohydrates every 20–30 min at the target race dose, progressively increasing intake over weeks until reaching 90–120 g/hr without GI distress.

---

Q: Why do some elite cyclists report tolerating 120 g/hr or more of carbohydrates?
A: Years of consistent gut training have maximally upregulated intestinal transporters and adapted gastric emptying, pushing their personal absorption ceiling above the population average.
